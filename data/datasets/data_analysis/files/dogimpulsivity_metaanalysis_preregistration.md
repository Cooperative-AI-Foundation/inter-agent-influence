Impulsivity as a trait in domestic dogs (*Canis familiaris*)

PRISMA-P Protocol

**SECTION 1: Administrative Information**

**Title**

Impulsivity as a trait in domestic dogs (*Canis familiaris*)

**Registration**

We are registering the protocol for our meta-analysis on the Open Science Framework (OSF) using a template that adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol (Moreau & Gamble, 2020). The protocol was registered on 14 June 2022.

**SECTION 2: Introduction**

Impulsivity is a multifaceted concept that typically captures an inability to wait, a preference for risky outcomes, a tendency to act without forethought, an insensitivity to consequences, and/or an inability to inhibit inappropriate behaviors (Evenden, 1999; Reynolds et al., 2006; Stevens, 2017). Impulsivity is particularly important for the domestic dog (*Canis familiaris*), as pet and working dogs must inhibit their impulses as a part of obedience training (Stevens et al., 2022). Over the last several years, dog researchers have investigated whether impulsivity is a behavioral trait of dogs by assessing whether individual dogs show consistency in impulsivity across multiple tasks (Bray et al., 2014; Fagnani et al., 2016; Brucks, Marshall-Pescini, et al., 2017; van Horik et al., 2018). The impulsivity tasks tested include A-not-B, cylinder, detour, and wait-for-treat tasks (Olsen, 2018). Many of these studies do not show consistency in behavior across these tasks. Therefore, we want to take a meta-analytic approach to assess the evidence for impulsivity as a behavioral trait in dogs.

The objective of our study is to conduct a meta-analysis of studies measuring dogs' performance in multiple impulsivity tasks to compare overall relationships between tasks and assess the evidence for a behavioral trait of impulsivity.

There are two main aims of this meta-analysis:

1. Examine studies in domestic dogs that measure impulsivity in multiple tasks to find which tasks have been studied together and whether behavioral responses correlate between tasks.

2. Investigate relationships between behavioral measures of dog impulsivity and owner perceptions of dog impulsivity using the Dog Impulsivity Assessment Scale (DIAS).

**SECTION 3: Methods**

Eligibility criteria

Studies will be collected according to the criteria outlined below:

**Design**

Only complete, published papers that are available in an electronic format will be included in the analysis. Both experimental and correlational studies will be included in our meta-analysis; observational work will not be included. While conference proceedings may be included, conference abstracts will be excluded from the meta-analysis. Relevant dissertations will be included; however, if duplicates exist (i.e., the dissertation was later published as a paper), the published paper will be the one included in the review. Furthermore, we will only include studies if we are able to convert the reported effects into correlation coefficients.

**Participants**

We will only analyze primary research papers that use tasks to measure impulsivity in domestic dogs, excluding any research papers examining only wild canids. Studies investigating impulsivity in wild canids may be included if domestic dogs are also included; however, we will only analyze the data from domestic dogs. For the purpose of our review, domestic dogs will include pet dog, working dogs, and free-ranging dogs. There will be no restrictions on the ages of the dogs used in the studies. We will use studies with dogs of both sexes, as well as both intact and neutered (including spayed) dogs.

**Time frame and language**

We will include studies published in 2003 onwards. According to Bensky et al. (2013), the first paper investigating impulsivity in dogs was published in 2003. Therefore, that is where we will begin our search. We will only include articles that are available in the English language.

Information sources

We will search the PsychINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for research articles. In addition, we will scan the reference lists of the research papers in our final set of included studies to ensure literature saturation.

Search strategy

We will search the databases using the following search terms in the topic field (includes title, abstract, keywords) or in a full text search (also see Table 1 below):

(canine OR dog OR dogs) AND (impulsiv* OR inhibit* OR discount* OR "delayed gratification" OR "delay of gratification" OR "self control" OR "self-control").

During the search process, we will also filter out papers published before 2003. For the databases PsychINFO and Scopus we will use the date range 2003-2022. When using Web of Science we will set the date range as starting on 01 January 2003 and ending on 31 December 2022.

Duplicate papers will then be filtered out based on DOI or journal information using R programming, and remaining papers will be screened using article titles and abstracts. We will remove any papers that: 1) are not complete, original research papers available in English, 2) do not use domestic dogs as the study subjects, 3) do not include either two impulsivity tasks or one impulsivity task and the DIAS, or 4) do not report the statistics required to obtain a correlation coefficient. Study records

We will import the output from the searches into R to merge the results and remove duplicates. We will then create a spreadsheet with the relevant information for each entry, along with columns signaling whether to include or exclude the entry (along with a reason for exclusion).

Two independent reviewers will complete the screening of title and abstract for all entries, excluding review papers, conference abstracts, incomplete or unpublished reports, papers not available electronically, and those not available in the English language. Reviewers will also exclude studies that do not use domestic dogs as the study subjects. Each reviewer will enter in the spreadsheet if the screening criteria are met for each entry. Reviewers will log the reason for exclusion. If there is a disagreement between reviewers, a third reviewer will adjudicate that entry in consultation with the other reviewers.

For the full paper eligibility, two independent reviewers will both complete screening for 20 randomly chosen entries looking for studies containing either two or more tasks that measure impulsivity in dogs, or one or more impulsivity task in addition to the use of the DIAS. Studies that do not meet these requirements will be excluded. Studies will also be excluded if they do not report their sample size, in addition to a correlation coefficient, standardized beta, or some other effect size that shows an association and can be converted to a correlation coefficient. Effect sizes must be provided for dogs specifically, not across several species. During this process, reviewers will log why entries were excluded. They will then extract and record the relevant data (measure names for pair [tasks or DIAS], sample size for pair, effect size for pair) for included studies. The reviewers will then compare their responses to look for discrepancies in coding the data. They will meet with a third reviewer to discuss whether the exclusion criteria and inclusion coding are sufficiently calibrated. Once the reviewers are calibrated, two reviewers will split the remaining entries to code, unless there are 50 or fewer entries, in which case they both will code all entries.

To extract data, reviewers will read the Methods, Results, Discussion, and/or Supplementary Materials sections of the papers to search for correlations between two different impulsivity tasks or between an impulsivity task and the Dog Impulsivity Assessment Scale (DIAS) overall score (sometimes called OQS). They will record the effect sizes (typically correlation coefficients) and sample sizes for each pair of impulsivity tasks or DIAS/impulsivity tasks.

Data items

Correlational measures

The first aim of this meta-analysis is to examine correlations between tasks used to measure impulsivity in dogs. For this aim, the correlational measures will consist of the tasks used to measure impulsivity in each research paper. To be included in the analysis, the study must utilize at least two separate measures of impulsivity. Impulsivity tasks that may be used include, but are not limited to, the cylinder task, detour-fence task, leash task, delay-discounting, wait-for-treat task, reversal task, and A-not-B task (Olsen, 2018). The second aim of this meta-analysis is to investigate correlations between impulsivity tasks and measures of perceived dog impulsivity from the owner, guardian, or handler of the dogs. The correlational measures for this aim are tasks used to measure impulsivity (same as above), as well as the Dog Impulsivity Assessment Scale (DIAS). To be included in the analysis for this aim, the study must use at least one measure of impulsivity in addition to utilizing the DIAS.

Study and population characteristics

We will record the following study and population characteristics for each study included in the final analysis:

- Type of domestic dog: pet, working, or free-range
  - We will also make note of whether a specific breed of dog or type of working dog is used as the subject (i.e., all Border Collie, all police dogs, etc.)
- Sex of the subjects, and the number of females/males
- Neutered status of dogs, if available (including spayed)
- Impulsivity task(s) used
- Whether the DIAS was utilized
- Whether the authors reported effects as being significant

Data synthesis

The first step in our analysis will be to convert any other forms of effect sizes to correlation coefficients. If this is not possible, the study will be excluded.

The primary type of synthesis will be two tables. One table will be a matrix of the impulsivity tasks within each cell of (half of) the matrix, we will list the correlation coefficient, the sample size, and the citation for each study that correlates that pair of tasks. This will likely be a sparse matrix. The second table will be for the DIAS/impulsivity task data. It will have a single column of effect size, sample size, and citation with each row representing a DIAS/task combination.

If any of the cells in either of the tables has three or more studies in it, we will conduct a robust Bayesian, model-averaged meta-analysis using the *RoBMA* package (Bartoš & Maier, 2020). We will input the correlation coefficients and sample sizes into the *RoBMA()* function in the *RoBMA* package, using default priors. This function allows us to test not only if there is evidence of an effect but also evidence for between-study heterogeneity (whether there is variation in true effect sizes across studies) and publication bias (Maier et al., 2022). This analysis uses model averaging to calculate (1) a Bayesian estimate of the effect size across all studies, (2) a Bayes factor for evidence supporting the hypothesis of an effect, (3) a Bayes factor for evidence supporting the presence of between-study heterogeneity, and (4) a Bayes factor for evidence supporting the presence of publication bias (Maier et al., 2022).

Meta-bias(es)

Using the *RoBMA()* function from the *RoBMA* package (Bartoš & Maier, 2020) will allow us to provide evidence for or against publication bias. In addition, the *RoBMA()* function provides measures of the precision-effect test (PET) and precision-effect estimate with standard error (PEESE), which assess how effect sizes are related to standard error---another measure of publication bias. We will report the Bayes factor for publication bias and both PET and PEESE estimates and 95% confidence intervals.
